# STP Framework — Application Template

## When to use
Developing a marketing strategy: deciding which customers to target and how to position the brand or product for them. Use when: entering a new market, launching a new product, or repositioning an existing brand.

## The three steps

### Step 1: Segmentation
Divide the total market into distinct groups of customers with similar needs, behaviors, or characteristics.

**Segmentation bases**:
- **Demographic**: Age, income, gender, education, occupation, family size
- **Geographic**: Country, region, urban/rural, climate
- **Psychographic**: Lifestyle, values, attitudes, personality, social class
- **Behavioral**: Usage rate, brand loyalty, purchase occasion, benefits sought, readiness to buy

**What makes a good segment**:
- **Measurable**: Can you quantify the size and purchasing power?
- **Accessible**: Can you reach this segment with marketing and distribution?
- **Substantial**: Is it large enough to be profitable?
- **Differentiable**: Does it respond differently to marketing mix than other segments?
- **Actionable**: Can you design an effective program to attract this segment?

**Output**: A list of 3-5 meaningful segments with characteristics described for each.

### Step 2: Targeting
Evaluate the attractiveness of each segment and choose which to serve.

**Evaluate each segment on**:
- Segment size and growth potential
- Competitive intensity within the segment
- Company's ability to serve this segment (capabilities, resources)
- Fit with company objectives and brand

**Targeting strategies**:
- **Undifferentiated**: Same offering for all segments (works when differences are minimal)
- **Differentiated**: Different offerings for multiple segments
- **Concentrated (Niche)**: One segment only (limited resources, high specialization)
- **Micromarketing**: Highly customized (local or individual level)

**Output**: State which segment(s) are targeted and why others were rejected.

### Step 3: Positioning
Define how the company wants to be perceived in the target customers' minds relative to competitors.

**Positioning statement format**:
> "For [target segment], [brand] is the [category] that [key benefit] because [reason to believe]."

**Positioning axes to consider**:
- Price vs. quality
- Functional vs. emotional benefits
- Traditional vs. innovative
- Mass vs. premium
- Local vs. global

**Perceptual map**: Plot the company and competitors on two relevant axes (e.g., price vs. quality) to show white space and differentiation.

**What to find**: How competitors are positioned, what the target segment values most, what the company can credibly claim.

## Output structure

**Segmentation**: Present the key segments as a table with demographic, psychographic, and behavioral characteristics.

**Targeting**: State the chosen segment(s) and the rationale. Quantify if possible (segment size, growth rate).

**Positioning**: Write the positioning statement. Describe or sketch the perceptual map.

## Final synthesis
Connect STP to the Marketing Mix (4Ps): How does the chosen positioning inform product features, pricing strategy, distribution channels, and promotion messages?

## Common mistakes
- Defining segments so broadly they're not actionable ("millennials" is not a segment — "urban millennials aged 25-34 with household income >$60K who prioritize sustainability" is)
- Targeting too many segments with limited resources — concentrated focus usually outperforms spreading thin
- Positioning on features rather than benefits — customers buy outcomes, not specifications
