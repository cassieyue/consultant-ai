# Blue Ocean Strategy — Application Template

## When to use
Identifying market spaces with limited competition where the company can make the competition irrelevant by creating new demand. Use when: the existing market is highly competitive (margins compressed, rivalry intense), incremental improvements are insufficient, or the company needs a step-change innovation strategy rather than a better version of the same offering.

## Core concept: value innovation

Blue Ocean Strategy is built on **value innovation** — simultaneously pursuing differentiation AND lower cost, not trading one off against the other. The goal is to create new demand rather than fight for existing demand.

This is distinct from:
- Differentiation alone (you still compete in the existing market space, just better)
- Cost leadership alone (you still compete for existing demand, just cheaper)

## The Four Actions Framework (ERRC Grid)

For each key competitive factor in the industry, ask:

| Action | Question | Financial effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Eliminate** | Which factors the industry takes for granted should be eliminated entirely? | Reduces cost |
| **Reduce** | Which factors should be reduced well below the industry standard? | Reduces cost |
| **Raise** | Which factors should be raised well above the industry standard? | Increases value |
| **Create** | Which factors should be created that the industry has never offered? | Creates new demand |

Eliminate + Reduce = cost reduction (frees resources)
Raise + Create = value increase (creates new reasons to buy)
Both together = **value innovation**

## Step 1 — Map the current value curve

Identify the 6–10 key factors that the industry currently competes on. Examples for airlines: price, meals, lounge access, seat comfort, on-time departure, frequent flyer rewards, hub connectivity.

Rate the average competitor on each factor (Low / Medium / High). This is the industry "value curve."

Then plot the company's current position. The question is: does your curve diverge from competitors, or does it look the same — just slightly higher or lower on the same factors?

## Step 2 — Apply ERRC

Work through each competitive factor and assign an ERRC action. Look especially for:
- Factors the industry invests in heavily that customers don't value — Eliminate or Reduce
- Factors customers consistently say are insufficient — Raise
- Jobs customers are trying to do that no one in the industry currently addresses — Create

**Target the non-customer**: Blue Ocean is about creating new demand. Who isn't currently buying in this industry, and what would it take to bring them in?

## Step 3 — Test the new value curve

A valid Blue Ocean strategy satisfies three criteria:
1. **Focus**: The new curve emphasizes 3–4 key factors, not all of them — a strategy that tries to win on everything wins on nothing
2. **Divergence**: The new curve diverges clearly from the industry average — overlay the two curves; if they track each other, you're competing in the red ocean
3. **Compelling tagline**: The strategy can be stated in one sentence that a non-customer would respond to ("Finally — [what they get] without [what they've always had to put up with]")

## Output structure

Present the ERRC grid with specific actions and rationale:

| Competitive Factor | ERRC Action | Specific Change | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Factor] | Eliminate / Reduce / Raise / Create | [What exactly changes] | [Why this creates value for non-customers or reduces cost] |

Then describe the new value curve and assess it against Focus, Divergence, and Tagline.

State: What new market space is being created? Who are the non-customers (people not currently buying in this industry) who would now buy? What is the estimated size of this new demand?

## Common mistakes
- Using Blue Ocean to justify incremental improvements — if you are only "Raising" factors, you are differentiating, not doing Blue Ocean
- Ignoring cost — value innovation requires both Eliminate/Reduce AND Raise/Create; only raising is premium positioning with higher cost
- Not identifying the non-customer — the analysis must name who is not currently buying and why; the ERRC grid is designed to address their reasons for not buying

## Combines with
- **Porter's Five Forces**: Run Porter's first to identify the red ocean forces you are trying to escape — high rivalry and intense buyer power are the forces that Blue Ocean bypasses; the ERRC grid often targets the factors driving those forces
- **Business Model Canvas**: ERRC decisions affect multiple BMC building blocks — creating a new factor (Create action) typically requires new Key Activities, Key Resources, and possibly different Customer Segments and Channels
- **SWOT**: Existing Strengths inform which Raise and Create actions are feasible now; current Weaknesses and commodity activities are candidates for Eliminate or Reduce
