# Business Model Canvas — Application Template

## When to use
Mapping and stress-testing a business model, or designing a new one. Use when: describing how a company creates, delivers, and captures value; evaluating whether a business model is coherent and viable; identifying where to innovate; or comparing two business models side by side.

## The nine building blocks

### Customer Segments
Who are we creating value for?
- Define the specific segments served (B2C, B2B, multi-sided platform)
- Prioritize: which segments are most important to the model?
- Avoid "everyone" — specificity is required. If multiple segments are served, describe each.

### Value Propositions
What value do we deliver to each customer segment?
- What customer problem do we solve or what need do we satisfy?
- Types of value: performance, design, price, convenience, status, risk reduction, accessibility, novelty
- What is the minimum viable value proposition — the reason a customer chooses this over alternatives?

### Channels
How do we reach, communicate with, and deliver to customers?
- Map the full customer journey: Awareness → Evaluation → Purchase → Delivery → After-sales
- Own channels (stores, website, sales force) vs. partner channels (distributors, platforms, retailers)
- Assess: reach, cost, control, and margin impact of each channel

### Customer Relationships
What type of relationship does each segment expect, and what does the model require?
- Personal assistance, dedicated account management, self-service, automated, community, co-creation
- Relationships range from fully human-mediated to fully automated — the choice affects cost and lock-in

### Revenue Streams
How does the company generate revenue from each customer segment?
- Asset sale, usage fee, subscription, licensing, advertising, brokerage, leasing, data monetization
- Pricing mechanisms: fixed, negotiated, auction, market price, volume-based
- What is the revenue contribution and margin of each stream?

### Key Resources
What assets are required to deliver the value proposition and make the model work?
- Physical (facilities, equipment, vehicles), Intellectual (brand, IP, data, algorithms), Human, Financial
- Which resources are owned vs. leased vs. accessed through partnerships?
- Which resources are hardest to replicate? (This is where competitive advantage lives)

### Key Activities
What activities must the company perform to operate this business model?
- Production, problem solving, platform/network management
- Which activities directly deliver the value proposition? Which support channel or customer relationship management?

### Key Partnerships
Who are the key partners and suppliers? What do we get from them?
- Strategic alliances, JVs, buyer-supplier relationships, co-opetition
- Motivations for partnership: optimization (reduce cost/risk), access to resources or markets
- Risk: which partnerships are the business most dependent on? What happens if a key partner fails?

### Cost Structure
What are the most important costs inherent in this model?
- Cost-driven (lean operations, low price value proposition) vs. value-driven (premium offering, high cost)
- Fixed costs (rent, salaries) vs. variable costs (materials, commissions)
- What are the top 3 cost items? Which scale well (reduce per unit as volume grows)?

## Output structure

Describe each block with specific evidence (not generic descriptions). Then run two checks:

**Fit check**: For each right-side block (VP, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams), ask: does this match what the Customer Segments actually want and are willing to pay for?

**Viability check**: Do the Key Resources and Key Activities cover what is needed to deliver the VPs? Are Revenues sufficient to cover the Cost Structure at achievable scale?

## Final synthesis
State: Is the business model coherent (all nine blocks align)? Is it differentiated (VP is meaningfully better than alternatives)? Is it viable (revenues > costs at scale)? What is the single biggest structural risk in the model?

## Common mistakes
- Listing activities and resources without connecting them to specific value propositions — every block should trace back to a customer job-to-be-done
- Ignoring unit economics — the BMC describes structure but not whether numbers work at scale
- Treating the BMC as static — it's most powerful as a before/after comparison (current model vs. proposed innovation)

## Combines with
- **Value Chain**: The Key Activities and Key Resources blocks map to value chain activities; use value chain analysis to add cost and performance detail to the BMC's operational blocks
- **SWOT**: Map BMC blocks to SWOT quadrants — strong VP and customer relationships → Strengths; thin resources or partner dependency → Weaknesses; untapped segments → Opportunities; competitor superior model → Threats
- **Blue Ocean Strategy**: Use Blue Ocean's ERRC grid to redesign the BMC — identify which VP elements and activities to Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, or Create to move into new market space
