# Porter's Five Forces — Application Template

## When to use
Assessing the competitive intensity and profitability potential of an industry. Use to decide whether to enter a market, why margins are high or low, and where competitive advantage must come from.

## The five forces

### 1. Threat of New Entrants
How easy is it for new competitors to enter the industry?

**Factors that raise barriers (lower threat)**:
- High capital requirements
- Strong brand loyalty / switching costs
- Economies of scale
- Proprietary technology or patents
- Government regulations / licenses required
- Access to distribution channels difficult

**Factors that lower barriers (higher threat)**:
- Low startup costs
- No switching costs for customers
- Easy access to distribution
- No regulatory protection

**What to find**: Capital required to enter, key regulations, brand strength of incumbents, customer switching behavior.

### 2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers
How much leverage do suppliers have over the industry?

**High supplier power when**:
- Few suppliers, many buyers
- Switching suppliers is costly
- Supplier's input is critical and has no substitutes
- Supplier could forward-integrate (become a competitor)

**Low supplier power when**:
- Many suppliers competing for business
- Commodity inputs with easy substitution
- Buyer is a large portion of supplier's revenue

**What to find**: Number of key suppliers, input substitutability, concentration ratios.

### 3. Bargaining Power of Buyers
How much leverage do customers have?

**High buyer power when**:
- Few buyers, many sellers
- Product is undifferentiated (buyers can shop around)
- Low switching costs
- Buyers could backward-integrate (make it themselves)
- Buyers are price-sensitive

**Low buyer power when**:
- Many fragmented buyers
- High switching costs
- Product is differentiated or essential

**What to find**: Customer concentration, switching costs, availability of alternatives, price sensitivity.

### 4. Threat of Substitute Products
Can customers meet the same need with a different product or service?

**High substitution threat when**:
- Substitute offers similar value at lower cost
- Switching to substitute is easy
- Substitute industry is growing and innovating

**Low substitution threat when**:
- No close substitutes exist
- Substitute requires significant behavior change
- Brand loyalty is strong

**What to find**: What else solves this customer problem, price-performance comparison of substitutes.

### 5. Competitive Rivalry
How intense is competition among existing players?

**High rivalry when**:
- Many competitors of similar size
- Industry growth is slow (companies fight for share)
- Products are undifferentiated
- Exit barriers are high (sunk costs)
- Fixed costs are high (pressure to fill capacity)

**Low rivalry when**:
- Few dominant players, clear market leader
- Industry growing rapidly (less zero-sum competition)
- Differentiated products reduce direct comparison

**What to find**: Number and size of competitors, industry growth rate, differentiation level, market share distribution.

## Output structure

Start with a summary table:

| Force | Key Evidence | Rating | Implication for [company/decision] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Threat of new entrants | [1–2 specific data points] | High / Medium / Low | [so what] |
| Supplier power | [1–2 specific data points] | High / Medium / Low | [so what] |
| Buyer power | [1–2 specific data points] | High / Medium / Low | [so what] |
| Threat of substitutes | [1–2 specific data points] | High / Medium / Low | [so what] |
| Competitive rivalry | [1–2 specific data points] | High / Medium / Low | [so what] |

Then expand each row: 2–3 sentences of supporting evidence and the strategic implication. Every claim must cite a specific data point — not "competition is intense" but "five players with near-equal market share and no differentiation, EBITDA margins compressed to 4% (IBISWorld 2024)".

## Final synthesis
**Overall industry attractiveness**: High / Medium / Low

State which 1–2 forces are most threatening and which represent opportunities. Then connect to strategy: how should the company position itself given these forces?

## Common mistakes
- Rating forces without evidence ("rivalry is high because there are many competitors" — how many? what's their market share?)
- Not connecting the analysis to a strategic implication
- Applying the framework to the wrong level (company vs. industry — this is an industry-level framework)

## Combines with
- **PESTEL**: PESTEL is the macro layer; Five Forces is the competitive layer. Run PESTEL first — macro factors (regulation, economic cycle, technology) shape the intensity of each force; combine both in the industry attractiveness conclusion
- **VRIO**: Five Forces tells you what competitive advantage you need; VRIO tells you whether the company actually has it. After identifying the most threatening forces, use VRIO to assess whether the company's resources can neutralise them
- **STP**: Competitive intensity and buyer power (from Five Forces) define where to target and how to position. High buyer power in mass-market segments may push positioning toward a niche where buyers have less leverage
