# SWOT Analysis — Application Template

## When to use
Summarizing the strategic position of a company before making a recommendation. Use as input to strategy generation — SWOT is a structured situation summary, not a strategy in itself. Most useful when combined with TOWS to convert the four quadrants into strategic options.

## The four quadrants

### Strengths (internal, positive)
What does the company do better than competitors, or what resources does it have that they lack?
- Proprietary technology, IP, or data
- Brand recognition and loyalty
- Cost advantages (scale, location, process)
- Talent and organizational capabilities
- Strong financial position
- Existing customer relationships and distribution

**What to find**: Company reports, analyst assessments, VRIO analysis output

### Weaknesses (internal, negative)
Where is the company underperforming relative to competitors or strategic requirements?
- Cost disadvantages
- Capability gaps
- Weak brand or reputation in the target segment
- Operational inefficiencies
- Financial constraints
- Dependencies on single suppliers, customers, or markets

**What to find**: Compare performance metrics against industry benchmarks; look for recurring themes in analyst critiques

### Opportunities (external, positive)
What market, environmental, or competitive trends could the company exploit?
- Growing segments or markets
- Competitor weaknesses
- Regulatory changes that favor the company's model
- Technological changes the company is positioned to leverage
- Underserved customer needs

**What to find**: PESTEL analysis, Porter's Five Forces, market research

### Threats (external, negative)
What external factors could harm the company's position?
- New competitors or substitutes
- Regulatory risk
- Economic downturns
- Shifting customer preferences
- Supply chain disruption risk
- Technological disruption

**What to find**: PESTEL analysis, Porter's Five Forces, industry reports

## TOWS — converting SWOT to strategy

After completing the four quadrants, generate strategic options using TOWS:

| | Strengths (S) | Weaknesses (W) |
|---|---|---|
| **Opportunities (O)** | SO strategies: Use strengths to exploit opportunities | WO strategies: Fix weaknesses to exploit opportunities |
| **Threats (T)** | ST strategies: Use strengths to mitigate threats | WT strategies: Minimise weaknesses and avoid threats |

For each cell, generate 1–2 specific strategic options. The best consulting output is TOWS-derived strategy, not just four lists.

## Output structure

Present the four quadrants with 3–5 bullet points each. Every point must be specific — "strong brand in Southeast Asia" not "strong brand." Then produce the TOWS matrix with specific strategic options (not generic descriptions). State which SO, WO, ST, or WT strategies are highest priority and why.

## Common mistakes
- Using SWOT as the conclusion rather than the input — "Our strength is X" is an observation, not a strategy
- Listing too many items in each quadrant — pick the 3–5 most strategically significant; quality over quantity
- Blurring internal and external: Strengths and Weaknesses must be internal (within the company's control); Opportunities and Threats must be external (market or environment)
- Not producing TOWS strategies — a SWOT without strategic recommendations is incomplete analysis

## Combines with
- **Porter's Five Forces**: Run Porter's first — the five forces output populates Threats and Opportunities directly (industry-level threats from rivalry, substitutes, new entrants = SWOT Threats; attractive forces = SWOT Opportunities)
- **McKinsey 7S / VRIO**: Use 7S or VRIO to populate Strengths and Weaknesses with organizational evidence rather than vague assertions — 7S identifies structural gaps, VRIO confirms which capabilities are genuine competitive advantages
- **Marketing Mix (4Ps/7Ps)**: TOWS strategies feed directly into Mix decisions — SO strategies often dictate product, price, and promotion choices for the target segment
