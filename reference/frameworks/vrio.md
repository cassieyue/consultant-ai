# VRIO Framework — Application Template

## When to use
Assessing whether a company's resources and capabilities can generate a sustainable competitive advantage. Use when: diagnosing why a company wins or loses, evaluating strategic options, or making a case for why a company is well-positioned for a new market.

## The four questions

### Valuable (V)
Does this resource or capability allow the company to exploit an opportunity or neutralize a threat?

- Does it reduce costs or increase revenues compared to not having it?
- Does it help the company meet a customer need?
- Does it allow the company to take advantage of market conditions?

If NO → The resource is a competitive disadvantage or irrelevant. Don't invest in it.

### Rare (R)
Is this resource controlled by only a small number of competitors?

- How many firms in the industry have this resource?
- If everyone has it, it's a necessity (competitive parity), not an advantage.
- If only 1-2 firms have it, it's rare.

If Valuable but NOT Rare → Competitive parity. The company won't lose, but it won't win either.

### Inimitable (I)
Is it costly or difficult for competitors to copy or acquire?

Sources of inimitability (why things are hard to copy):
- **Unique historical conditions**: First-mover advantage, legacy relationships built over decades
- **Causal ambiguity**: Competitors can see the resource but can't understand why it works
- **Social complexity**: Culture, trust, teamwork — impossible to replicate even if you understand it
- **Patents/legal protection**: Legal barriers to imitation

If Valuable + Rare but NOT Inimitable → Temporary competitive advantage. Competitors will catch up.

### Organized (O)
Is the company organized to capture value from this resource?

- Are the right systems, processes, and incentives in place to exploit this resource?
- A valuable, rare, inimitable resource still fails if management, structure, or culture doesn't support it.

If V + R + I but NOT Organized → Unused competitive advantage.

## Output structure

| Resource/Capability | Valuable | Rare | Inimitable | Organized | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Resource 1] | Yes/No | Yes/No | Yes/No | Yes/No | Sustained advantage / Temporary / Parity / Disadvantage |
| [Resource 2] | | | | | |

For each resource rated as Sustained Competitive Advantage (all four = Yes):
> **[Resource]**: [Evidence that it's V, R, I, O] → This is a core competitive advantage that should be protected and leveraged in the new market/strategy.

## Final synthesis
State: which 1-2 resources represent true sustained competitive advantages, and how the strategy should be built around them. Also flag any resources that are valuable but not organized — these represent internal improvement opportunities.

## Common mistakes
- Applying VRIO to products/markets rather than internal resources and capabilities
- Being too generous with "Rare" and "Inimitable" — most resources are more imitable than companies think
- Not specifying what "organized" actually means in practice (which systems or processes need to exist)

## Combines with
- **Value Chain Analysis**: Value chain identifies which activities the company performs and at what cost; use VRIO to test whether the activities with the highest strategic importance actually produce sustained competitive advantage — value chain identifies *what* to test, VRIO determines *whether it is defensible*
- **McKinsey 7S**: The 7S framework surfaces the organizational resources and capabilities (Staff, Skills, Shared Values, Systems) to test with VRIO; 7S tells you what the organization has, VRIO tells you which of those things are actual competitive advantages
- **Modes of Entry**: Entry mode selection should protect VRIO advantages — if the competitive advantage is inimitable technology or brand, a JV that shares IP with a local partner threatens the "I" condition; WOS preserves the advantage intact
