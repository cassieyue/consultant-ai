# Pyramid Principle — Application Template

## What it is
A communication structure developed by Barbara Minto at McKinsey. The core idea: start with the answer, then support it. Never make the reader wait for your conclusion.

This applies to every consulting deliverable: slide decks, reports, memos, emails, presentations, and verbal recommendations.

## The structure

```
ANSWER (the recommendation or conclusion)
├── Key Argument 1
│   ├── Supporting evidence / data
│   └── Supporting evidence / data
├── Key Argument 2
│   ├── Supporting evidence / data
│   └── Supporting evidence / data
└── Key Argument 3
    ├── Supporting evidence / data
    └── Supporting evidence / data
```

The answer sits at the top. Everything below it exists to prove the answer.

## The SCQA opening

Before the pyramid, set up WHY the question matters:
- **Situation**: What is undeniably true (facts everyone agrees on)
- **Complication**: What has changed, or what tension exists in the situation
- **Question**: What this raises — the question the reader is now asking
- **Answer**: Your recommendation — the top of the pyramid

Example:
- *Situation*: MG Motors currently operates in 16 countries across Europe.
- *Complication*: Competition from local EV manufacturers is intensifying, and European growth has stalled at 2% CAGR.
- *Question*: Where should MG expand next to sustain growth?
- *Answer*: MG should enter the Mauritius market via franchising within 12 months.

Then the rest of the document proves the Answer.

## Rules for each level

### Answer level
- One clear, specific, actionable statement
- Not: "There are several options to consider"
- Yes: "MG should enter Mauritius via franchise dealerships by Q3 2025"

### Key arguments (2-4 per answer)
- Must be MECE — no overlap, collectively they prove the answer
- Each must be a complete thought ("The Mauritius market is growing" not "Market growth")
- Group by: reasons why (inductive) or logical steps (deductive)
- Typically 3 is the right number — fewer feels thin, more is hard to remember

### Supporting evidence
- Specific data, not assertions ("GDP grew at 7%" not "the economy is strong")
- One source per claim
- The evidence directly supports the argument above it

## Applying to slides

**Slide header = the argument, not the label**

Every slide header should be a sentence stating the point of that slide:
- Bad: "Market Analysis" (label — tells reader nothing)
- Good: "Mauritius is one of Africa's fastest-growing markets, with 7% GDP growth and an 81/100 ease of doing business score" (argument)

If the reader could only read the headers and understand the full story, the deck is structured correctly. This is called the "ghost deck" test.

**Slide body** = evidence that proves the header

## Applying to written reports

Open with SCQA (above). Then:
- Each section heading = the key argument for that section
- First sentence of each section = restate the argument
- Body of section = evidence and analysis
- Last sentence = "so what" — how this supports the overall recommendation

## Inductive vs. deductive grouping

**Inductive**: Multiple independent facts that together support a conclusion
> "We recommend market X because: (1) strong GDP growth, (2) favorable regulation, (3) low competition"

**Deductive**: A logical sequence where each point leads to the next
> "EVs require charging infrastructure → Mauritius has 100% electricity access and is investing in charging networks → therefore, infrastructure is not a barrier to MG's entry"

Use inductive for recommendations (3 independent reasons). Use deductive for explaining mechanisms or causality.

## Common mistakes
- Building up to the conclusion (the "mystery novel" structure) — consultants never do this
- Key arguments that aren't actually independent (they overlap — not MECE)
- Evidence that proves something adjacent to the argument, not the argument itself
- Headers that describe what the slide contains rather than what the slide says
