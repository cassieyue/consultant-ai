# Modes of Entry — Application Template

## When to use
Deciding how a company should enter a foreign market. The goal is to match the entry mode to the company's risk tolerance, resources, need for control, and the target market's characteristics.

## The spectrum (low control/risk → high control/risk)

### 1. Exporting
Company produces at home, sells abroad (directly or through an intermediary).
- **Best for**: Testing a market, limited resources, high trade barriers are absent
- **Benefits**: Low investment, low risk, easy to exit
- **Drawbacks**: No control over distribution, low margins (intermediary takes cut), no local presence or brand building
- **Key question**: Are trade barriers low enough to make exporting cost-competitive?

### 2. Licensing
Company grants a foreign firm the right to use its IP (brand, technology, process) in exchange for royalties.
- **Best for**: Markets where local regulations require local production, IP can be transferred without losing competitive advantage
- **Benefits**: Rapid market penetration, no capital investment, royalty income
- **Drawbacks**: Loss of quality control, risk of IP leakage, licensee may become a competitor
- **Key question**: Can the company protect its IP legally in this market?

### 3. Franchising
Company (franchisor) grants franchisees the right to use its business model, brand, and support systems in exchange for fees and royalties.
- **Best for**: Service businesses, consumer brands where local knowledge matters, scaling fast across geography
- **Benefits**: Rapid expansion with low capital, local franchisee brings market knowledge
- **Drawbacks**: Quality consistency is hard to maintain, need to build franchisee support infrastructure, geographic distance complicates oversight
- **Key question**: Is the business model easily replicable by a franchisee? Can quality be standardized?

### 4. Joint Venture (JV)
Company creates a new entity with a local partner, sharing ownership, control, and risk.
- **Best for**: Markets requiring local knowledge, regulatory environments that favor local partnerships, high-capital projects
- **Benefits**: Shared risk and cost, local partner brings knowledge and connections, may be required by law
- **Drawbacks**: Shared control (disagreements), risk of IP transfer to partner, misaligned objectives over time
- **Key question**: Is there a trustworthy local partner available? Does the market require local partnership?

### 5. Wholly Owned Subsidiary (WOS)
Company owns 100% of the foreign operation. Two routes:
- **Greenfield**: Build from scratch
- **Acquisition**: Buy an existing local company

- **Best for**: Full control needed, proprietary processes must stay in-house, strong brand standards required
- **Benefits**: Full control over operations, strategy, and IP; all profits retained
- **Drawbacks**: Highest cost, highest risk, slowest to establish, need to understand local market alone
- **Greenfield vs. Acquisition**: Acquisition is faster but comes with integration risk and cultural baggage

## Decision framework

Ask these questions in order:
1. **Does local law restrict foreign ownership?** → If yes, JV or licensing may be required
2. **How important is control over quality/IP?** → High = lean toward WOS; Low = can franchise or license
3. **What are the capital constraints?** → Limited capital = exporting, licensing, or franchising
4. **How much local market knowledge is needed?** → High = JV or local acquisition
5. **What is the risk tolerance?** → Low = exporting or licensing; High = WOS greenfield

## Output structure

Evaluate the top 2-3 modes most relevant to this situation:

> **[Mode]**: [Why it fits or doesn't fit this specific situation] → **Verdict**: Recommended / Possible / Not recommended — [reason in one sentence]

State the chosen mode clearly and explain why the alternatives were ruled out. Don't just say "JV has benefits and drawbacks" — say "JV was rejected because there are no suitable local partners in this industry and the company's EV technology is a core competitive asset that cannot be shared."

## Common mistakes
- Describing each mode generically without applying it to the specific company and market
- Not ruling out alternatives explicitly — the strongest analysis explains why NOT, not just why yes
- Ignoring legal constraints on foreign ownership (always check this first)
